# PIECE #1 — "The DBA Documentation File"
**Stage:** Decision · **Format:** Long-form guide + downloadable template · **Target reader:** Rahul

---

### If you enrol in a doctoral program and it goes wrong later, the problem will not be the school. It will be the folder you didn't keep.

That's the uncomfortable lesson of the last six months. On 12 August 2026, SEVP told every designated school official in the country that it is seeing a rise in practical-training authorisations that don't meet the regulatory standard — training that isn't "an integral part of an established curriculum" — and that it is increasing scrutiny of those approvals. It reminded DSOs that when they sign your I-20, they certify that information under penalty of perjury.

Read that again, because most people miss the consequence: the burden of proof doesn't land on the school. It lands on you, years later, in an H-1B or green-card file, when an officer asks what your practical training actually was.

This guide is the folder you should have kept. Five documents and one email, before you pay anything.

---

## 1. What "an integral part of an established curriculum" actually means

The phrase comes from **8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(i)**, and it is the whole ballgame. Curricular Practical Training is permitted when the training is:

- **an integral part of an established curriculum**, and
- **directly related to your major area of study**, and
- **not authorised solely for the purpose of employment**

Three conditions. All three have to be true at the time the training happens — not at the time someone assured you it would be fine.

For graduate students, the regulation allows practical training to begin immediately, from the first term, when the curriculum genuinely requires it. That's the basis on which Day-1 CPT programs exist. It is a real provision, not a loophole.

**What the August 2026 broadcast did — and did not do.** It restated the standard above and said SEVP is increasing scrutiny of approvals that don't meet it. The message states its own limits: it is a compliance reminder to schools, **not a rule and not a ban on Day-1 CPT.** DHS had already confirmed, in the D/S final rule, that it "does not prohibit or eliminate Day-1 CPT."

That distinction is the whole reason this guide exists. Nothing was banned. The documentation standard simply got enforced.

**The one pattern that creates the most exposure:** finding a job first, then enrolling and attaching a course credit to it afterwards. The regulation requires the curriculum to require the training — not the other way around. If the sequence was job → course, no folder will fix the underlying question.

---

## 2. The five documents to collect before you pay a deposit

### Document 1 — The written practicum requirement
The catalog page or curriculum sheet showing that practical training is **required for every student in the program** — not offered, not available, not "an option." Required.

**Why it matters:** this is the document that answers the first question an officer will ask. Save the dated catalog page and the URL. If the requirement changes in a later catalog year, you want the version that applied to you.

### Document 2 — The syllabus that links the training to your major
The course syllabus for the term in which you'll do the practical training, showing the learning outcomes tied to your field of study.

**Why it matters:** "integral" and "directly related to your major" are both answered by a syllabus — not by a job title on an offer letter.

### Document 3 — Your CPT authorisation record
Your I-20 showing the CPT authorisation, your employer's name, the start and end dates, and the hours per week. Keep every version — each authorisation is typically limited to a term and renewed.

**Why it matters:** a mismatch between what your SEVIS record says and what your employer's file says is one of the few problems that cannot be explained away afterwards. If they don't match, fix it while you're still enrolled.

### Document 4 — The job description, mapped to your major
Your offer letter or job description, plus a one-paragraph written explanation of how the day-to-day work relates to your field of study. Written by you, at the time, not reconstructed three years later.

**Why it matters:** this is the document people most often cannot produce when asked. Reconstructing it from memory in an RFE response is materially weaker than producing it from the file.

### Document 5 — Enrollment and attendance evidence
Proof of full-time enrolment each term, residency or on-site attendance records, and your transcripts.

**Why it matters:** the practical-training question is usually part of a wider "did you maintain status" question. Attendance and enrolment records answer the wider question, not just the narrow one.

> **Keep them somewhere you'll still have access to in five years.** Not a university portal login that expires, and not a work laptop you'll hand back. A folder you control.

---

## 3. The email to send your DSO — exact wording

Send this **before** you pay a deposit, and save the reply with your documents. If a school won't answer these questions in writing before you enrol, treat that as your answer.

> Subject: Prospective DBA student — requesting written confirmation of practicum and CPT policy
>
> Dear [DSO name],
>
> I'm considering the [program name] and want to confirm the practical-training structure before I apply.
>
> Could you confirm, in writing:
>
> 1. Is the practical training/practicum requirement applicable to **every student** in this program, and where is that stated in the curriculum?
> 2. Is the training **directly related to the student's major area of study** as structured in the program?
> 3. Which course or credit structure does the training attach to each term?
> 4. What documentation will I be asked to keep, and can I have a copy of the school's CPT policy?
>
> I'd also like to understand the residency or on-site attendance requirement for my intake.
>
> Thank you,
> [Name]

**Why this works:** it is a reasonable, professional request that any well-run program answers easily. The quality of the reply tells you more about the program than any brochure.

---

## 4. What your employer has to sign, and when

Three things, in this order — and ideally before your first day of work under CPT:

1. **An offer letter or job description** with duties specific enough to map to your major. "Business analyst" alone is thin. "Business analyst — data modelling and reporting for the operations division" is defensible.
2. **A confirmation of hours and the work site**, matching exactly what goes into your SEVIS record. If either changes — hours, location, duties — update the record. A material change that nobody documented is the problem.
3. **A supervisor's expectation**: that you'll ask for a short written confirmation of duties each term. It's a two-line email from them, and it's worth more than a paragraph you write yourself later.

Your employer does not authorise CPT. **The school does.** Your employer's job is to be accurate, on paper, at the time.

---

## 5. What gets asked for later

Immigration practitioners report that scrutiny shows up downstream rather than at the point of authorisation — most often in H-1B petitions, change-of-status applications, and adjustment files, where requests for evidence ask for transcripts, syllabi, and proof that the curriculum required the off-campus training from the outset.

Two practical points follow:

- **Keep the folder current.** Adding each term's syllabus and confirmation while it's fresh takes five minutes. Reconstructing four years of them takes weeks and never comes out as strong.
- **Understand the OPT interaction.** Twelve months or more of full-time CPT — generally 20+ hours per week — eliminates OPT eligibility at that degree level. It's cumulative across your F-1 history and it cannot be reversed. Whether that trade is right for you is a decision to take with an immigration attorney, not a counsellor, and not a brochure.

**On choosing a program at a higher degree level:** moving from a master's to a doctorate changes your degree level, which is why some students consider it. Whether it suits your situation — and what it does to your long-term options — is a question for licensed immigration counsel. Anyone who tells you it's an upgrade with no downside is selling, not advising.

---

## 6. What this file does not do

- It is **not immigration legal advice.** For anything affecting your status, speak to a licensed immigration attorney.
- It is **not a guarantee.** Future Wise does not authorise CPT, does not decide admissions, and cannot promise an approval or an outcome.
- It is **not a substitute for the school's own policy.** Ask the school, get it in writing, keep the reply.
- It is **not complete without dates.** Every document is worth more when it's dated and matches what was filed at the time.

Anyone in this market offering you a guarantee is offering you something they cannot deliver. The folder is what you can actually control.

## The only question that decides this comparison

Forget rankings for a moment. Forget tuition. Forget the brochure photographs of a campus you'll visit twice a year.

There is one structural question that separates these four programs in every way that matters after you graduate: **how is the practical training built into the curriculum — and can the school show you that in writing?**

Everything below is arranged around that question. Where a detail is unconfirmed by the university's own published material, this page says so rather than filling the gap. You're going to verify all of it anyway, and a comparison page that admits its gaps is worth more than one that appears complete.

## What we compared, and what we didn't

We're comparing four institutions commonly considered by working professionals researching a doctorate in business. All four are US institutions offering a DBA in formats that combine online coursework with periodic on-site attendance.

**We are not comparing:**
- Rankings (they measure things that don't affect your documentation)
- Which is "best" (that depends entirely on your degree history, your target intake, and your situation)

## How each handles practical training — and what you must verify yourself

This is where a comparison table stops being useful. None of the four can be assessed from a webpage, because the answer depends on your specific program, your specific term, and your specific employment.

**Ask each of them these four questions, in writing:**

1. Is the practical training/practicum **required for every student** in this program, or is it an available option?
2. Where is that requirement stated in the curriculum — and can I have that page?
3. Which course does the training attach to each term?
4. Can I have a copy of the school's CPT policy before I pay a deposit?

Compare the four replies side by side. **The quality and speed of the reply is the most useful data point on this page** — more useful than tuition, more useful than the accreditation line, and far more useful than anything a counsellor tells you on a call.

## Where the risk sits

To be direct about it: the risk in any of these programs is not the institution. It's the sequence — **finding a job first and then attaching a course credit to it.** The regulation requires the curriculum to require the training, not the other way around.

If a program's structure can't be explained to you in plain language, that's information. Ask again, and if it still can't be explained, that's your answer.

## How to verify all of this yourself in about 20 minutes

1. Open the regulation: **8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(i)**. It's short. Read the words "integral part" and "directly related."
2. Open each university's program page and find the curriculum or catalog entry.
3. Search that page for the practicum requirement. **Is the word "required" there, or is it "may"?**
4. Email the DSO the four questions above and time the reply.

You don't need an advisor for that. What you might want an advisor for is comparing the answers — which is what the Fit Report does, using your degree dates, your status and your target intake.

# PIECE #3 — "What the August 2026 SEVP Memo Does and Doesn't Change"
**Stage:** Awareness · **Format:** Explainer · **Target reader:** Sneha

---

## What actually happened on 12 August 2026

On 12 August 2026, SEVP sent a broadcast to designated school officials. In it, SEVP said it has observed a rise in CPT authorisations that don't meet regulatory requirements — specifically training that does not constitute "an integral part of an established curriculum" — and that it is **increasing its scrutiny of CPT approvals.**

That's it. That's the news. Everything else you've read in a group chat is someone's interpretation.

## What it restates

The broadcast restates the existing standard from **8 CFR 214.2(f)(10)(i)**: practical training may be authorised only when it is an integral part of an established curriculum and directly related to your major area of study, and it cannot be authorised solely for the purpose of employment.

It also reminds DSOs that when they sign or re-sign your I-20 to authorise CPT, they are certifying that information is true — including that the training is integral to your curriculum. Improper authorisation can put the school's SEVP certification, and the individual official, at risk.

None of that is new. It's the rule that has applied all along.

## What it does not do

This is the part that matters most, so it's worth stating as plainly as possible:

- **It is not a rule.** The broadcast says so itself.
- **It is not a ban on Day-1 CPT.** DHS confirmed in the D/S final rule that it "does not prohibit or eliminate Day-1 CPT."
- **It does not retroactively invalidate an authorisation** that your DSO properly issued.
- **It does not mean every international student with practical training is now in trouble.**

If you've seen a post claiming SEVP "banned Day-1 CPT," that post is wrong. Scrutiny and prohibition are different things, and the difference is the entire story.

## So why does it matter?

Because scrutiny means **documentation.** Practitioners report that scrutiny tends to surface downstream — in H-1B petitions, change-of-status applications and adjustment files — where requests for evidence ask for transcripts, syllabi, and proof that the curriculum required the off-campus training from the outset.

So the practical effect isn't that your options closed. It's that **the evidence standard is being enforced, and the evidence has to be yours.**

## The test that follows from it

One question, applied to whatever you're considering:

> **Can the school show you, in writing and in advance, that the practical training is required for every student in your program and directly related to your major?**

If yes, you're in a structurally sound position — and you should keep the documents that prove it. If no, or if nobody will answer the question clearly, that's the signal worth acting on.

## What to do next

1. **Don't panic and don't sign anything this week.** Nothing here creates a deadline.
2. **Ask the four questions.** They're in our DBA Documentation File guide, with an email you can copy.
3. **Start the folder now**, even before you choose a program. Every term you document is one you don't have to reconstruct later.
4. **Take status questions to a licensed immigration attorney.** Not a counsellor, not a group chat, not us. We're an education advisory, not your lawyer — and any advisor who doesn't say that clearly is worth less than one who does.
