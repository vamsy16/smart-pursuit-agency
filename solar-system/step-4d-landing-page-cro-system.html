Section 01

Why Landing Pages Matter

Ads generate traffic. Landing pages convert traffic. These are two different jobs, and they fail independently of each other.

An agency can build a well-structured campaign, write relevant ads, and win the click at a sensible cost — and still produce nothing, because the page the click lands on does not do its half of the work. This is the most common place a Solar campaign quietly loses money, and it is also the place clients are least likely to look when results disappoint.

The governing idea of Step 4D

Good advertising cannot compensate indefinitely for a poor landing page.

Spending more can mask a weak page for a while. It cannot fix it. Every additional rupee flows through the same broken step, so the waste scales with the budget rather than being absorbed by it.

Section 02

What Is CRO?

What it is

Conversion Rate Optimization is the process of improving a website or landing page so that a greater percentage of relevant visitors complete the desired action. The emphasis on relevant matters: the goal is not to convert everybody, it is to convert more of the people who should be converting.

Why it matters for Solar

Solar is a considered purchase with a long decision cycle and a high ticket value. Traffic is expensive and the buyer is cautious. Improving the page improves every campaign pointed at it at once — without raising media spend.

How it works

Observe behaviour, find where people hesitate or leave, form a hypothesis about why, change one thing, measure the result, keep what works. It is a loop, not a project.

What Smart Pursuit should do

Build every Solar page against the architecture in Section 6, instrument it with the events in Section 27 from day one, and run the audit in Section 32 before launch and monthly afterwards. Without tracking in place first, CRO becomes guesswork.

For Solar, the desired action could be

Solar assessment requestLead form submissionPhone callWhatsApp enquirySite assessment booking

A page may legitimately support several of these. What it must not do is present them with equal weight — that forces the visitor to make a decision about how to enquire before deciding whether to enquire. One primary action, one or two clearly secondary ones.

What CRO is not

The common misreading

"Make the page look better."

Redesigns driven by taste change everything at once, which means nothing is learned and any improvement cannot be attributed.

The working definition

Understand visitor behaviour → identify friction → test improvements → measure conversion → improve.

Changes are deliberate, isolated where practical, and judged against data rather than opinion.

Friction, in practical terms

Friction is anything that makes the next step harder than it needs to be. On a Solar page it usually appears as one of these:

Type of frictionHow it shows up on a Solar landing page
CognitiveVisitor cannot tell what the company does, or what "assessment" involves
TrustNo verifiable company information, no address, unverifiable claims
EffortTwelve form fields when five would qualify the lead adequately
TechnicalSlow load on mobile data, form that fails silently, broken WhatsApp link
AnxietyUnclear what happens after submission, or fear of aggressive sales calls
Beginner note

CRO does not require expensive software to begin. For a first Solar client, the ordered priority is: correct event tracking, then a page built to a sound structure, then observation of where people drop off, and only then formal A/B testing. Testing without traffic produces confident conclusions from insufficient data, which is worse than not testing.

Section 04

The Core Landing Page Offer

Primary offer across all Solar landing pages

FREE SOLAR ASSESSMENT

Why this, and not a direct sale

A rooftop Solar system in India is a significant capital decision — typically a multi-lakh purchase, installed on the buyer's own roof, with a payback period measured in years. Nobody makes that decision from an advertisement. Asking them to is asking for a commitment the visitor is not in a position to give.

Asks for too much, too early

Buy Solar Panels Now

Requires the visitor to have already decided on solar, on a system size, on a budget and on a supplier. The overwhelming majority have not.

Matches where the visitor actually is

Get a Free Solar Assessment

Asks only for a conversation about suitability. Low commitment, genuinely useful, and a natural first step toward a purchase.

The assessment works as an offer because it is the step the buyer actually needs next. It is not a marketing device wrapped around a sales call — it answers a real question the homeowner cannot answer alone: will this work on my roof, and what would it involve?

What the assessment can help the prospect understand

AreaWhat gets clarified
Electricity usageCurrent consumption pattern and how much of it solar could realistically offset
Property typeWhether an independent house, villa, shop, office or commercial building changes the approach
Rooftop suitabilityAvailable area, orientation, shading from adjacent buildings or water tanks, structural condition
Approximate system requirementAn indicative capacity range based on usage and available roof area
Installation considerationsAccess, mounting approach, wiring routes, meter and inverter placement
Potential savings considerationsThe factors that would determine savings — not a promised figure
Next stepsWhat a site visit, design and quotation process would involve
Non-negotiable compliance rule

Never present estimated savings as guaranteed savings. Every savings reference on a Smart Pursuit Solar page must be framed as potential, indicative or illustrative, and must be accompanied by the factors it depends on — consumption, tariff, system design, generation and site conditions. This applies to page copy, calculator output, ad copy and sales scripts equally.

Secondary offers worth considering

The assessment remains primary, but for some traffic a lighter alternative can capture visitors not yet ready to speak to anyone. Recommendation

  • Savings estimate via calculator — self-service, no contact required, captures the email only if the visitor chooses.
  • Rooftop suitability check over WhatsApp — lower perceived commitment than a form, suits mobile-first Meta traffic.
  • Callback at a chosen time — addresses the visitor who wants to talk but not right now.

Each additional offer adds a decision for the visitor. Add one only if there is a clear reason, and never let a secondary offer compete visually with the primary CTA.

Landing Page Types

Three pages cover the realistic traffic sources for a Solar client. Each exists because the visitor arrives in a measurably different state of mind.

Page 1 — Solar AssessmentMeta & general campaigns

/solar-assessment

Primary purpose: capture assessment leads from Meta and general campaigns.

Visitor state: was not searching for solar. Saw something in a feed that raised the possibility. Needs the problem framed before the offer will mean anything.

Implication: more education, stronger visual communication, the problem stated before the solution. Navigation stripped so the only paths are convert or leave.

Page 2 — Solar InstallationGoogle Search

/solar-installation

Primary purpose: capture high-intent Google Search traffic.

Visitor state: actively looking. Typed something specific. Already past the "is solar worth it" stage and into "who should do this for me".

Implication: confirm relevance immediately, lead with service and process rather than education, make contact options prominent and immediate.

Page 3 — Local SolarLocation-specific search

/solar-installation-hyderabad

Primary purpose: capture location-specific Solar searches.

Visitor state: looking for someone who works in their area. Proximity and local credibility are the deciding factors.

Implication: name the specific areas served, reference local conditions honestly, make the service radius unambiguous.

On location pages — the most common mistake

A location page must be genuinely useful for that location. It should not simply duplicate content with the city name changed. Swapping "Hyderabad" for "Pune" across an otherwise identical page produces thin, near-duplicate content that serves the visitor poorly and gives search engines no reason to rank either page.

What makes a location page genuinely local

ElementWhat real localisation looks like
Areas servedNamed localities the client actually covers — for a Hyderabad client that might be Secunderabad, Rangareddy, Medchal, Sangareddy. Never list areas the client cannot service.
Local contextRegional factors that genuinely differ: typical property types in that market, local climate patterns affecting generation, the relevant state distribution utility.
Regulatory framingReference that net metering and approvals are handled through the applicable state utility and that requirements vary by state — without stating specific rules that may change.
ProofWhere the client has genuine completed installations in that city, reference them with verified details. Where they do not, say nothing rather than implying coverage.
ContactLocal office address if one exists. A real address is a strong local trust signal.
Scaling rule

Build a location page only where the client can genuinely service the area and where there is enough search demand to justify it. Three strong location pages outperform fifteen thin ones — in ranking, in conversion, and in the credibility of the client's own business.

Page length should follow traffic source and intent

The exact page length should depend on traffic source and user intent. Do not force unnecessary sections onto every landing page — each section the visitor must scroll past is a small cost, justified only when it does work.

Ordering principle

The sequence answers the visitor's questions in the order they naturally arise: What is this? → Does it apply to me? → How does it work? → What will it cost me to find out? → Can I trust these people? → What if I have concerns? → How do I start? Reordering sections tends to fail because it answers questions the visitor has not asked yet.

Section 08

Hero Design

Visual direction

Use

  • Real Indian residential rooftop Solar imagery
  • Indian home or property context — recognisable architecture
  • Professional Solar technician at work, where appropriate
  • Clean composition with space for the headline to sit legibly
  • Strong, visually dominant CTA
  • Minimal distraction around the primary action

Avoid

  • American or European suburban housing — visibly wrong market
  • Obviously AI-generated panels with impossible geometry
  • Cartoon or vector solar illustrations on a lead-gen hero
  • Generic stock-business imagery: handshakes, boardrooms
  • Busy collages that leave no clean area for text
  • Carousels and auto-playing video in the hero slot

The hero image is a credibility signal before it is a decorative one. An Indian homeowner comparing suppliers reads an American suburban roof as evidence the company is not local — which is precisely the opposite of what a location-targeted campaign needs to establish.

Imagery integrity rules

Do not use fake customer photos or fake project imagery. If a photograph is stock or representative rather than the client's own work, it must never be captioned or implied as a completed project by that company.

Where representative imagery is used on a demonstration build, label it plainly — for example "Representative Solar installation examples" — and state that the images are not presented as the company's own projects.

Claims that must not appear unless verifiable

10,000+ customers500+ installations15 years experienceBest Solar companyIndia's #1 Solar companyMW installedAward-winningGovernment approved partner

None of these may be used unless the actual client can verify them and would stand behind them in writing. This is not only an ethical position — unverifiable superlatives are a disclosure risk, they invite competitor complaints, and Indian Solar buyers have become notably sceptical of them.

What to use instead when the client is new

A newer Solar company is not without credible things to say. Verifiable substance beats invented scale: registered company details, a real office address, named service areas, the actual installation process, the specific warranty terms offered, the panel and inverter brands supplied, and the qualifications of the installation team. These are checkable, which is exactly what makes them persuasive. Section 11 develops this.

Message Match

One of the most important CRO principles, and one of the cheapest to fix. The visitor should immediately recognise that they reached the page they expected.

ADSets an expectation
↓
LANDING PAGEMust confirm that expectation within about five seconds

Matched examples

Meta

Ad: Get a Free Solar Assessment

Page: Get Your Free Solar Assessment

Near-identical phrasing. The visitor recognises it without reading carefully.

Google

Ad: Solar Installation in Hyderabad

Page: Solar Installation in Hyderabad

Exact match including the city. The search term, the ad and the H1 form one continuous line of thought.

A mismatched example

What creates friction

Ad: Free Solar Assessment

Landing page: Welcome to XYZ Energy — India's Leading Solar Company

Three separate problems are created at once:

  1. The promised offer has vanished. The visitor clicked for an assessment and arrived at a company introduction. They must now search the page for the thing they were promised.
  2. The subject has changed. The ad was about the visitor's property. The page is about the company. Attention shifts from "what do I get" to "who are these people", which is a weaker starting position.
  3. An unverifiable claim opens the relationship. "India's Leading Solar Company" invites scepticism at the exact moment the page should be building confidence.

The cost is not only lost conversions. On Google, landing page experience is a component of Ad Rank, so poor message match can raise what the client pays per click while simultaneously reducing what each click is worth.

Match at four levels

LevelWhat must alignCommon failure
WordingHeadline echoes the ad's core phrasePage uses a company slogan instead of the offer
OfferThe thing promised is the thing availableAd offers an assessment, page pushes a product catalogue
VisualHero image resembles the ad creativeAd shows an Indian rooftop, page shows a solar farm
LocationCity in the ad appears in the pageCity-targeted ad points to a generic national page
Smart Pursuit working rule

Before any Solar campaign goes live, open each ad next to its landing page on a phone screen. Read the ad headline, then the page H1. If the connection is not obvious in a single glance, the match is not close enough. This five-minute check catches more lost conversion than most optimisation work performed afterwards.

Every roof is different. Available area, shading, orientation and your actual electricity usage determine whether solar suits your property and what size system would be appropriate. 2. Initial discussion. A specialist calls to understand your usage pattern and what you are hoping to achieve.

Benefits

Copy Patterns Worth Reusing

The hedging vocabulary

Solar marketing copy lives or dies on how claims are framed. These substitutions keep the page persuasive while remaining defensible:

Do not writeWrite instead
Save 90% on your electricity billReduce the electricity you draw from the grid — potential savings depend on your usage and system size
Guaranteed savings of ₹5,000 per monthIllustrative savings range, based on stated assumptions
Eliminate your electricity billOffset a portion of your daytime consumption
Get government subsidy of ₹78,000Subsidy eligibility depends on applicable government policies, regulations, location and system requirements
Payback in 3 yearsPayback period varies with consumption, tariff, system cost and generation
Works on every roofSuitability depends on available area, shading, orientation and structural condition
Subsidies and incentives — standing rule

Never publish specific subsidy amounts, scheme names with figures, or eligibility promises on a landing page. Government schemes change, vary by state and system size, and are frequently misreported. The safe formulation: "Eligibility for government incentives depends on applicable policies, regulations, your location and system requirements. We can explain what may apply to your property during the assessment." Accurate, useful, and it moves the conversation to the call — which is where it belongs.

Sentence-level habits that make copy read as human

Do

  • Use "your roof", "your bill", "your property"
  • Name specifics — shading, orientation, roof area
  • Admit limits: "usually not entirely"
  • Keep paragraphs to three or four lines on mobile
  • Use plain numbers: 3 kW, not "three kilowatt peak"

Avoid

  • Stacked adjectives: "premium world-class expert"
  • Exclamation marks in body copy
  • Emoji in headings on a considered-purchase page
  • Repeating the same benefit in three sections
  • Jargon without explanation: "net metering", "kWp"

Explaining technical terms inline

Solar copy unavoidably contains technical vocabulary. Explain it in the sentence rather than in a glossary nobody opens:

  • kW / kWp — "a 5 kW system, which is a common size for an independent house"
  • Net metering — "net metering, where excess generation you export is credited against what you draw from the grid, subject to your state utility's rules"
  • Inverter — "the inverter, which converts the panels' output into the power your home uses"
  • Generation — "generation, meaning the units the system actually produces, which varies with season and weather"
A test for finished copy

Read the page aloud. Anywhere the phrasing would be awkward to say to a homeowner standing in front of you, rewrite it. Landing page copy that cannot be spoken naturally is usually copy written to impress rather than to inform — and on a high-value considered purchase, that reads as evasive.

Form Optimization

Short form

Lower friction. Typically more submissions. Less information per lead, so the sales team qualifies on the call instead.

Longer form

Potentially better qualification. Typically fewer submissions. More context before the first call, and self-selection filters out casual enquiries.

The objective

The objective is not the shortest form. It is the best balance between conversion volume and lead quality.

Section 22

Common Solar Objections

Every objection the page fails to address becomes a reason not to enquire. These are the recurring six in the Indian residential and small-commercial market.

"Will Solar work for my property?"

How to answer: This is exactly what the assessment determines. Suitability depends on available roof area, shading from surrounding structures, orientation and the structural condition of the roof. Rather than claiming it works everywhere, the page should position the assessment as the way to find out — including the possibility that the answer is no.

"How much Solar do I need?"

How to answer: System size depends on electricity usage and property conditions. The bill band in the form and the calculator both give the visitor a rough orientation, while making clear that an accurate figure requires a site evaluation.

"Will Solar actually save me money?"

How to answer: Savings depend on usage, tariffs, system design, generation and other factors. Explain the mechanism — self-generated units reduce grid draw — and be explicit that the extent varies. Never answer this with a guaranteed figure or a fixed payback period.

"How much roof space is required?"

How to answer: Requirements depend on system size and panel and system specifications. Panel efficiency, mounting configuration and layout constraints all affect it. Give the principle rather than a universal number.

"What happens after I submit the form?"

How to answer: Describe the process explicitly — who calls, roughly when, what will be discussed, and that there is no obligation. This is the cheapest objection to resolve and one of the most frequently neglected. Section 4 of the page and the thank-you page should both cover it.

"How long does installation take?"

How to answer: Do not provide a universal number unless it has been verified for the specific client's process. Timelines vary with system size, site conditions, material availability and approval processes. If the client can commit to a typical range for their own work, state it as typical and note what can extend it.

Objection handling principle

Answer the objection honestly, then route to the assessment. The pattern is consistent: acknowledge that it depends, explain what it depends on, and position the assessment as the way to get a specific answer. This is both accurate and commercially effective — it makes the assessment feel necessary rather than promotional.

CRO Formula

Landing Page Conversion Rate
Number of Conversions ÷ Landing Page Visitors × 100

Worked example

If a page receives 1,000 visitors and produces 50 leads:

50 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 5%

The landing page conversion rate is 5%.

Read this carefully

The figures above are a mathematical example demonstrating the formula. They are not a Solar benchmark, not a target, and not an expected result. Smart Pursuit has no Solar conversion rate data, and any agency quoting a standard Solar landing page conversion rate without reference to a specific account, traffic source and offer is guessing.

Definitional care

  • Count conversions consistently. Decide whether WhatsApp and phone clicks count as conversions, then apply it uniformly. Mixing definitions between months makes trends meaningless.
  • Use landing page sessions, not total site sessions. Including other pages understates the page's actual performance.
  • Segment by device and source. A blended rate can hide a mobile problem entirely.
  • Exclude internal and test traffic. Agency and client visits during build distort early figures noticeably at low volumes.
Section 30

A/B Testing

A/B testing shows two versions of a page to comparable audiences and measures which performs better against a defined goal. Its purpose is to replace opinion with evidence — particularly useful when agency and client disagree about a change.

Test one major variable at a time where practical. Changing several things at once may produce a result, but it will not explain which change caused it, so nothing transferable is learned.

TestVariableWhat it investigates
1Headline A vs Headline BWhich framing of the offer resonates
2Long form vs short formThe volume / quality trade-off from Section 17
3Assessment CTA vs Consultation CTAWhich word carries less perceived commitment
4Hero image A vs hero image BWhether imagery affects credibility and engagement
5Form above fold vs form lower on pageWhether the visitor needs context before being asked
6Savings calculator vs no calculatorWhether interactivity aids or delays conversion
Not every test produces a winner

Many tests end inconclusive, and that is a legitimate outcome — it means the variable did not matter much, which is itself worth knowing. Use sufficient data before drawing strong conclusions. With the traffic volumes typical of a single Solar client, expect tests to run for weeks rather than days, and expect to test only changes substantial enough to produce a detectable difference. Testing button colour on 400 visitors a week is not CRO; it is noise collection.

Section 31

CRO Priority Framework

Most CRO effort is misallocated toward small changes that are easy to make. This framework forces attention to what actually constrains conversion.

High ImpactFix immediately
  • Broken form — submissions failing, validation blocking valid input, leads not arriving
  • Wrong CTA — the primary action does not match what the traffic wants
  • Poor mobile experience — unusable on the device most visitors use
  • Slow page — visitors leaving before the page renders
  • Ad / page mismatch — the promise in the ad is absent from the page
  • Unclear offer — visitor cannot tell what is being offered or to whom

Each of these can suppress conversion substantially on its own. None require testing — they require fixing.

Medium ImpactTest deliberately
  • Headline testing — framing and emphasis
  • Form length — the volume / quality balance
  • CTA wording — "assessment" versus "consultation"
  • Trust placement — where proof appears relative to the form

Worth structured testing once high-impact issues are resolved and traffic is sufficient.

Low ImpactAddress opportunistically
  • Minor font changes
  • Small spacing adjustments
  • Decorative changes

Fine to improve while doing other work. Never the answer to "leads are down".

Priority rule

Fix major conversion blockers before optimizing cosmetic details.

Prioritising within a tier

Where several items sit at the same level, rank them on three questions: How many visitors does this affect? How severely does it affect them? How quickly can it be fixed? A mobile issue affecting 70% of traffic outranks a desktop issue affecting 30%, even if the desktop issue is more visually obvious in the agency's own browser.

A pattern worth recognising

When a client asks for a redesign because "the page isn't converting", run the Section 21 audit first. The constraint is frequently a single high-impact item — a broken form notification, a slow hero image, a mismatch between ad and page. Fixing it costs an afternoon. A redesign costs weeks and rebuilds the same problem in a new layout.

Smart Pursuit's CRO Principle

The principle

Don't optimize a Solar landing page to look better. Optimize it to make the next decision easier for the right visitor.

Two words carry the weight. Decision — because the visitor is not browsing, they are deciding whether to spend a significant sum on their own roof. And right — because converting the wrong visitor produces a lead the sales team wastes time on and a cost the client absorbs.

The sequence a page must support

What is this?Understood within five seconds of arrival
↓
Is it relevant to me?My property type, my city, my situation
↓
Can I trust it?Verifiable company, honest claims, real detail
↓
What happens if I enquire?Who calls, when, and what it commits me to
↓
What should I do next?One obvious action, easy to take

Every section in the architecture exists to answer one of these. A section that answers none of them is decoration, and decoration on a lead-generation page is a cost paid in scroll depth and attention.

A test for any proposed change

Before making a change, identify which of the five questions it helps answer, and for whom. If the answer is "it looks more modern", the change may still be worth making — but it should not be expected to affect conversion, and it should not be prioritised ahead of something that does.
